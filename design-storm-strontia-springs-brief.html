The Strontia Springs Spike

Two nights ago, a sensor on Denver Water’s own supply watched a river go from clear to filthy in about an hour, and then clean itself up again by lunchtime. All of it is public, and it is the phenomenon Cassidi’s team is trying to predict.

Why this exists

What I was doing when you asked

Cassidi described a model her team is building, in one paragraph, on a call. I wanted to see whether the thing she described actually shows up in public data, and what it looks like when it does.

It does, on Denver Water’s own supply, and there was a live example two nights before we spoke. This is what that looks like and what makes it interesting, nothing more. Deciding whether any of it becomes a conference exercise is a separate question, and Cassidi’s to answer first.

Her words

The problem Cassidi described

What he’s trying to do is basically look at certain water quality parameters. And model based on storm events where we might see exceedances. So you could plug in any type of storm event or stream flow and say, this is where we might have a problem for future… planning, and then that kind of informs those who work on our water quantity, how we move water, where we take water from, and then subsequently our treatment plants, what they need to do to treat the water. Cassidi Rosenkrance, 17 Aug 20261

Unpacked: a storm dumps rain on a mountain watershed. Soil, ash and organic matter wash into the streams that feed the reservoirs. The water arriving at the treatment plant is briefly much dirtier than usual. If you can predict when and how dirty, you can move water from a cleaner source instead, or tell the plant to change how it treats what’s coming.

Vocabulary

Every term you need, once

Watershed
The land area that drains into a particular river or reservoir. Rain falling anywhere in it eventually shows up in that water body. Not a building.
Gage
A fixed monitoring station in a river. The USGS spelling of “gauge.” Instruments take readings automatically, here every 15 minutes, and publish them free.
Turbidity FNU
How cloudy the water is, measured by shining a light through it and reading how much scatters sideways. Clear mountain water sits near 2. Above roughly 100 the river looks like coffee. FNU is Formazin Nephelometric Units.
Discharge cfs
How much water is flowing past a point, in cubic feet per second. This is what people mean by “stream flow.”
Specific conductance µS/cm
How well the water conducts electricity, which tracks how much dissolved mineral is in it. Rainwater is nearly pure, so a big rain event usually dilutes a river and pushes this number down.
Exceedance
A reading that goes past a threshold somebody cares about, whether a legal limit or an operational one like “past this, our plant can’t cope.” Which thresholds Denver Water uses is still an open question.
Provisional data
Published immediately, before a human has reviewed it. Everything in this brief is provisional. It may be revised or withdrawn later.2
Design storm
A hypothetical rainfall event of specified size that engineers design drainage against. Also, now, the name of our format, which turns out to be more literal than we intended.

The find

One gage sits on Denver Water’s own supply

USGS station 06707525, South Platte River above Strontia Springs Reservoir, publishes turbidity, specific conductance, water temperature, pH and dissolved oxygen every 15 minutes, free and unauthenticated.3 Strontia Springs is a Denver Water reservoir, and it is the one with the story: after the 1996 Buffalo Creek and 2002 Hayman fires, storms washed more than a million cubic yards of sediment into it, roughly fifteen years’ worth in a single flood season. Denver Water has spent over $27 million on the consequences, and the episode is what started their forest-and-watershed program.45

Two more of their collection watersheds carry the same instruments: the Fraser River below the Moffat Tunnel (09023562) and the Williams Fork near Parshall (09037500). Across Colorado there are 49 active turbidity series and 362 discharge series running right now.3

So the domain has public instruments pointed directly at Denver Water’s drinking water, with a famous disaster attached to explain why anyone cares.

The connection

How this gage relates to Denver

The gage is about 38 km (24 miles) south-west of downtown Denver, up a canyon, which makes it look remote on a map. Functionally it is the opposite of remote. It sits immediately above Strontia Springs Reservoir, and Denver Water says plainly: “Eighty percent of Denver Water’s supply passes through Strontia Springs Reservoir.”8

Denver is on the dry side of the mountains, so the city reaches west for water and then funnels almost all of it through one narrow point before treatment. That point is this reservoir, and this gage is the last measurement taken before the water gets there.

What makes it hard

Three difficulties hiding in thirty-one rows

  1. The obvious predictor doesn’t work

    Anyone modelling “storm makes river dirty’’ reaches first for flow. Flow never moved: 139–147 cfs across the entire event, while turbidity went up 30×. Whatever delivered that sediment came in below the upstream gage, from a tributary or a burn scar, and the headline measurement is blind to it.

  2. There is a lie in the data and nothing marks it

    At 13:00 on the 14th, conductance reads 38, sitting between neighbours of 296 and 300. It carries no error flag, only the same “provisional” label every other reading carries.2 Almost certainly a sensor artifact, but nothing in the feed says so.

  3. The shape is asymmetric

    Turbidity rose roughly 30× in about an hour and then took nine hours to come back down. Rivers dirty fast and clean slow. Any model that treats the rise and the recovery as the same process gets the operationally important part wrong, because the recovery is the part that decides how long a treatment plant is dealing with it.

None of these are obvious from the description of the model. They only show up once you look at an actual event.

What the record shows

How this storm compares, and what actually breaches a standard

The Upper South Platte has been studied after fire, and USGS did it in cooperation with Denver Water. On the mainstem between Cheesman and Strontia Springs, in the year after the Hayman fire: “During rainstorms, some turbidity values exceeded 1,000 NTU… which is the maximum range for the equipment used to measure continuous turbidity in this study.”16 Against a pre-fire baseline that “averaged 5 NTU and did not exceed 10 NTU in most streams”.17 So the 303 FNU above is a real event and an ordinary one for this watershed. It pegged no instruments.

Turbidity is not the only candidate. In the one study built around how much of each basin burned, nitrate is its equal: basins burned severely across more than 45% of their area had “twice the streamwater nitrate and four times the turbidity as basins burned to a lower extent”.17

And here is the turn. If an exceedance means breaching an actual standard, the parameters that did so in this watershed were trace elements, not turbidity. Counting against the standards of the day, in 41 samples from a Hayman-burned tributary USGS recorded iron over its chronic standard 33 times, mercury 18, manganese 9, arsenic once.16 (The arsenic comparison used 50 µg/L, which is the pre-2006 federal drinking water limit rather than a current Colorado in-stream standard, so treat that one as dated.) On the mainstem the pattern shifts, and the report explains why: “the water-quality standards apply primarily to dissolved-phase… constituents, and the predominant phase associated with Hayman postfire runoff is particulate”.16 Dissolved manganese exceeded nothing there. What moved was particulate iron.

The one exceedance statement that names this reservoir directly is a seasonal load rather than a storm concentration: the 2003 April-September phosphorus load at the Trumbull gage “would have exceeded the allowable phosphorus load of 17,930 pounds (includes all sources upstream from Strontia Springs Reservoir outlet) for Chatfield Lake”, at almost 23,000 pounds.16

Worth knowing which rules apply where. The river through Waterton Canyon is not its own segment: it is part of COSPUS06A, “Mainstem of the South Platte River from the outlet of Cheesman Reservoir to the inlet of Chatfield Reservoir”, classified for Agriculture, Aquatic Life Cold 1, Recreation E and Water Supply. The reservoir itself is a different segment, COSPUS19, which additionally carries a direct use water supply classification and chlorophyll standards the river does not.19 A sample from the canyon and a sample from the pool are judged against different tables.

Two cautions for anyone counting exceedances here. Colorado’s metals standards are “levels not to be exceeded more than once every three years on the average”, so a single sample above a number is not a regulatory exceedance. And the manganese figure is not a fixed 50 µg/L: the standard is the less restrictive of that or the water’s existing quality as of January 1, 2000.19 The reach carries one live water-supply listing, and it is for arsenic, not for anything storms move. Strontia Springs Reservoir is not listed for anything at all.21

A correction to my own earlier note. I wrote that manganese was the real problem after Hayman. That claim belongs to the 1996 Buffalo Creek fire, not Hayman, and its only public sources are a USGS photograph caption and a 2002 university press release quoting a research assistant.18 The peer-reviewed statement for this watershed is far more guarded: fine sediment, organic matter and manganese “may decrease the efficiency of treatment processes”. Manganese is a treatment concern with a thin evidentiary base here, not the documented headline I made it.

What “exceedance” means

Not one threshold, and not always a legal one

I had assumed an exceedance meant breaching a legal limit. The published literature uses the word in two senses at once, on opposite sides of the same model, and the threshold being crossed is as often an operating limit as a regulatory one. Towler and colleagues, modelling exactly this problem for Portland’s drinking water source, put it plainly: water quality thresholds are “set by regulatory agencies or identified as limits to particular treatment options.”14

Then a harder fact, which settles it for turbidity specifically. Colorado sets no numeric in-stream standard for turbidity at all. The word does not appear in a single segment table in Regulation 38. What the state says about suspended material is narrative only: waters shall be free from substances that “can settle to form bottom deposits detrimental to the beneficial uses”, with no number.19 Turbidity is regulated instead at the combined filter effluent, the treated water leaving the plant, at 0.3 NTU for 95% of monthly readings and 1 NTU maximum under conventional filtration.20

So a raw-water turbidity reading in Waterton Canyon cannot legally exceed anything, in either direction. There is no in-stream limit, and the plant limit applies to water that has already been treated. The 303 FNU in the chart above is an operational signal about treatability, and nothing else. My original framing of it as a possible compliance number was wrong in kind, not merely in detail.

On the driver side, exceedance probability is an ordinary hydrology term for how often a storm or flow of a given size occurs. On the response side, it means a concentration crossing a fixed number. The numbers that matter operationally are not drinking water standards at all: conventional treatment is typically required above 20 NTU turbidity or 4 mg/L dissolved organic carbon, and manganese becomes a problem at 0.2 mg/L because it promotes microbial growth in reservoirs and pipes, well above the 0.05 mg/L secondary standard.15

There is also a published model that matches the description closely. It takes a precipitation forecast, generates streamflow scenarios, and returns the probability of crossing a turbidity threshold rather than a predicted value. The reason is instructive: at high flows the turbidity response scatters too widely to predict a number, but the probability of crossing the line is still forecastable, and that is what operations actually needs.14 Portland’s response to a likely exceedance is to switch to a more expensive groundwater source. The output of such a model is a decision, not a measurement.

Two things in that literature bear directly on the chart above. Turbidity serves as both a modelled target and a predictor of other constituents, which is a documented source of confusion and one I had fallen into. And post-fire response is driven by 30-minute rainfall intensity rather than storm total, so a short, violent cloudburst matters more than a long soaking. Whether the 15 August event was one of those is not something the gage data alone can tell us.

Limits

What I know, and what I am guessing at

Established

  • The phenomenon is real and observable on public instruments, on Denver Water’s own supply, without asking them for anything.
  • Storm event in, water quality out is exactly the direction Cassidi described.
  • The decisions she named (where to draw water from, how to treat it) are real operational consequences, which is what makes the prediction worth having.
  • This gage sits on the main supply line, immediately above the reservoir that 80% of Denver Water’s supply passes through.

Guesswork

  • Which parameters her data scientist actually models. Still her statement to make, though turbidity now looks like a reasonable guess rather than a shot in the dark: see below. Nitrate is its equal in the one study built around burn extent.
  • What counts as an exceedance and whose threshold it is. Partly settled: in this literature the threshold is as often a treatment-plant operating limit as a regulatory one, and “exceedance” carries a statistical sense as well as a compliance one. See below. Which of those Denver Water means is still hers to say.
  • Which watershed the model covers. Strontia Springs is my inference from her mention of a watershed program, not something she said.
  • Whether her model is predictive or diagnostic, and over what horizon. “Plug in a storm event” suggests a scenario tool, but that is reading a lot into one sentence.
  • Where the watershed program’s public data actually lives. She said the model runs on data “publicly available through our watershed program”. I could not find what that programme publishes or where. Everything in this brief comes from USGS instruments and Denver Water’s documents, not from that source, so there may be a body of data here I have never seen.

The items on the right are the ones only she can settle, and the last is a plain question worth asking.
